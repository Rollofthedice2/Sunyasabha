                         THE MODAL CATUṢKOṬI:
 FORMALIZING EMPTINESS IN CLASSICAL MODAL LOGIC

                                      CHANCE CHAPMAN

ABSTRACT: Nāgārjuna’s arguments in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā rely extensively on
modus tollens, yet this inference is invalid in the paraconsistent frameworks typically used to
formalize the catuṣkoṭi. We develop a modal extension of Belnap-Dunn semantics to address
this, expressed as: ¬□(v(P) = t) ∧ ¬□(v(P) = f) ∧ ¬□(v(P) = b) ∧ ¬□(v(P) = n). This formula
applies self-reflexively, resolves longstanding interpretive debates regarding verses within
Nāgārjuna’s Mūlamadhyamakakārikā and Vigrahavyāvartanī such as his “no thesis” defense,
and bridges interpretive gaps between traditionally designated Eastern and Western logical
traditions, providing a Western analytical translation of Indian dialectic into symbolic modal
logic that is system-agnostic across K, T, S4, S5, non-normal logics, and neighborhood
semantics. The modal operators function classically while the four-valued structure is
modalized, preserving modus tollens at the level of modal reasoning while accommodating
paraconsistent valuation at the object level. Self-application produces a non-terminating
regress that is virtuous rather than vicious: each application is self-contained, demonstrating
the unavailability of stable ground rather than constituting a failure to find it.

Keywords: catuṣkoṭi, Madhyamaka, modal logic, Nāgārjuna, paraconsistent logic, svabhāva

                                    1. INTRODUCTION

Central to philosophical Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism is the Madhyamaka
tradition, traditionally held as founded by the ancient Indian thinker Nāgārjuna.
Madhyamaka incorporates a distinctive argumentative and analytical structure known
as the catuṣkoṭi, or tetralemma in order to discuss the concept of ‘svabhāva’ – inherent
metaphysical essence or existence independent from outside alteration. The catuṣkoṭi
consists of four sides, or lemmas: [X] has svabhāva; [X] does not have svabhāva; [X]
both does and does not have svabhāva; [X] neither has nor does not have svabhāva.
Svabhāva, as inherent existence or essence independent of causes and conditions, is
intrinsically a modal property; to possess svabhāva is to possess one's nature
necessarily (Westerhoff 2009, 22).

    Graham Priest in “The Logic of the Catuskoti” (2010) has explored how the
catuṣkoṭi operates within paraconsistent four-valued logic (using true, false, both, and
neither) to avoid the consequences of the principle of explosion in classical and
intuitionistic logic. However, Nāgārjuna made extensive use of modus tollens, which
is invalid under Priest’s paraconsistent four-valued logics. This paper offers a modal
extension of Belnap-Dunn semantics to resolve this problem. By reasoning classically
to refute modal necessity-claims, we can employ modus tollens without restraint in a
system-agnostic fashion, valid across K, T, S4, S5 and extending naturally to non-
normal modal logics and neighborhood semantics, where analogous results hold under
their respective interpretations of necessity.

    We express this as the formula:

    ¬□(v(P) = t) ∧ ¬□(v(P) = f) ∧ ¬□(v(P) = b) ∧ ¬□(v(P) = n)

    The modal operators function classically; the four-valued structure is what is being
modalized. This preserves modus tollens at the level of modal reasoning while
accommodating paraconsistent valuation at the object level. By negating the necessity
claims of all truth-value assignments, the formula achieves self-application, embracing
the lack of a halting condition to analysis. This provides an internally consistent
explanation for Nāgārjuna’s statement within the Vigrahavyāvartanī (The End of
Disputes) in which he seems to assert the absence of a thesis. The formula, as
generalized method, applies to every positive assertion - including the assertion of the
formula itself - without reaching contradiction or explosion.

                                           2. THE FORMULA

2.1 COMPONENTS AND INTERPRETATION

It is necessary to define the components of the formula for the purposes of
disambiguation.
Let P be any proposition.
Let v be a valuation function assigning truth values to propositions, where v(P) ∈ {t, f,
b, n}:

    • t: true (supported by evidence, not refuted)
    • f: false (refuted by evidence, not supported)
    • b: both (supported and refuted)
    • n: neither (neither supported nor refuted)

    These are the four values of Belnap-Dunn semantics (Belnap 1977; Dunn 1976),
originally developed for reasoning with incomplete and inconsistent information.
Let □ denote the necessity operator of standard modal logic, where □φ asserts that φ
holds necessarily (in all accessible worlds under Kripke semantics, or under analogous
interpretations in other frameworks).
Let ¬ denote classical negation; let ∧ denote conjunction.

2.1.1 FORMATION RULES

The well-formed formulas of the language are defined recursively as follows:

    1. If P is a propositional variable and x ∈ {t, f, b, n}, then v(P) = x is a formula.
    2. If φ is a formula and x ∈ {t, f, b, n}, then v(φ) = x is a formula.
    3. If φ is a formula, then □φ is a formula.
    4. If φ is a formula, then ¬φ is a formula.
    5. If φ and ψ are formulas, then φ ∧ ψ is a formula.
    6. If φ and ψ are formulas, then φ → ψ is a formula.

    Propositional variables enter the language only through valuation atoms v(P) = x.
No formula asserts P independently of a valuation context, reflecting the dependence
structure central to the Madhyamaka analysis and preventing svabhāva-claims at the
level of P assertions.

We define φ ∨ ψ as ¬(¬φ ∧ ¬ψ) and ◇φ as ¬□¬φ.

    Models M = ⟨W, R, V⟩ consist of a set of worlds W, an accessibility relation R,
and a valuation function V assigning to each propositional variable P and world w a
value V(P, w) ∈ {t, f, b, n}.

2.1.2 SEMANTIC CLAUSES

The truth conditions for formulas at a world w in model M are defined recursively:

    1. M, w ⊨ v(P) = x iff V(P, w) = x, where P is a propositional variable.
    2. M, w ⊨ □φ iff for all w' such that wRw', M, w' ⊨ φ.
    3. M, w ⊨ ¬φ iff M, w ⊭ φ
    4. M, w ⊨ φ ∧ ψ iff M, w ⊨ φ and M, w ⊨ ψ
    5. M, w ⊨ φ → ψ iff M, w ⊭ φ or M, w ⊨ ψ

    For formulas φ other than valuation atoms:

    6. M, w ⊨ v(φ) = t iff M, w ⊨ φ
    7. M, w ⊨ v(φ) = f iff M, w ⊭ φ
    8. M, w ⊨ v(φ) = b holds at no world.
    9. M, w ⊨ v(φ) = n holds at no world.

    Clauses 8 and 9 follow from 6 and 7: since the satisfaction relation is bivalent (for
any φ and w, either M, w ⊨ φ or M, w ⊭ φ), exactly one of v(φ) = t and v(φ) = f holds
at each world. The values b and n are therefore inapplicable to formulas other than
valuation atoms. This asymmetry of being four-valued for atomic propositions and
bivalent for complex formulas does not affect the framework's argumentative work:
modus tollens at the modal level operates on the bivalent satisfaction of ¬□-formulas,

regardless of whether the underlying atomic variable carries a four-valued assignment.
The four-valued apparatus permits paraconsistent valuation of object propositions but
does not enter the inferential chain that refutes necessity.

2.1.3 DEFINITION

The modal catuṣkoṭi formula T(P) is:

          T(P) =def ¬□(v(P) = t) ∧ ¬□(v(P) = f) ∧ ¬□(v(P) = b) ∧ ¬□(v(P) = n)

         T(P) asserts: no truth value is necessarily assigned to P.

2.2 SELF-APPLICATION

Importantly, the formula applies to itself. As T(P) is itself a proposition, T can be
applied to T(P), like so:

     T(T(P)) = ¬□(v(T(P)) = t) ∧ ¬□(v(T(P)) = f) ∧ ¬□(v(T(P)) = b) ∧ ¬□(v(T(P)) = n)

    This asserts: the truth value of the claim "P has no necessary truth value" is itself
not necessarily any of the four values.

    This process can continue reflexively as T(T(T(P))), and so on, without a halting
point. Self-application is well-formed by formation rule 2, and the truth conditions for
v(T(P)) = x are given by semantic clauses 6–9. Because T(P) asserts something about
V(P) - not about V(T(P)) - no liar-like circularity arises. The non-terminating regress
reflects the structure of the analysis, not paradox.

    This prevents the formula from stabilizing into a necessary meta-position. One
cannot assert 'all propositions lack necessary truth values' as a necessary truth, as that
assertion is itself contingent - true in some models, false in others. This establishes the
non-necessity as structural rather than a defect to be remedied.

    More precisely: for any natural number n, the n-fold self-application Tⁿ(P) is
satisfiable in modal systems that admit asymmetric accessibility: K, T, S4, and their
non-normal extensions. At each stage, the formula is contingent in these systems, and
the next application therefore has the semantic space to operate.

    Crucially, n-fold self-application fails to be satisfiable in S5. This restriction is
principled rather than arbitrary: S5’s equivalence relation forces modal claims to be
class-constant within equivalence classes, which collapses the directional structure that
grounding relations require. That is, if worlds can access each other’s alternatives, the
truth of necessity-claims becomes fixed across them uniformly. Since svabhāva is
precisely a grounding-style necessity – necessity in virtue of intrinsic nature,
independent of conditions – it is not appropriately modeled by the uniform necessity of
S5. The formula’s failure to iterate in S5, rather than being a defect, is a formal
registration of the incompatibility between uniform necessity and the asymmetric
dependence relations characteristic of svabhāva. The n-fold satisfiability result for the

appropriate modal systems is formally verified in Lean 4 (see Appendix). This
accommodates Nāgārjuna’s rejection of all four lemmas so far as they relate to

    This self-referential structure requires that T(P) remain within the scope of the
analysis rather than quarantined at a separate metalinguistic level. Such refusal of type-
theoretic stratification is standard in paraconsistent treatments of self-reference (Priest
1987) and is philosophically necessitated: a rigid level-hierarchy would presuppose the
stable ground the formula shows to be unavailable.

    Formulas of the modal language receive values in {t, f} only, as a consequence of
semantic clauses 6–9; the four values {t, f, b, n} apply to propositional variables
through the base valuation V. When T applies to modal formulas like T(P), the
conjuncts concerning b and n are trivially satisfied, and T(T(P)) reduces to classical
contingency: T(P) is neither necessarily true nor necessarily false. This asymmetry is
philosophically appropriate: the method operates within conventional discourse, which
is bivalent; what the method reveals is that its object lacks necessary truth value. This
mirrors the Two Truths structure already invoked: conventional reasoning about
ultimate emptiness.

2.3 RELATION TO PRIOR FORMALIZATIONS

Graham Priest in In Contradiction (1987) articulated and defended the principle of
dialetheism, in which the negation of a true statement can also be true in non-classical
logical systems, such as paraconsistent logics. Priest has since elaborated extensively
on paraconsistent logics, notably in An Introduction to Non-Classical Logic (2001).

    In “The Logic of the Catuskoti” (2010), Priest applied this framework directly to
Nāgārjuna's tetralemma, demonstrating how paraconsistent four-valued semantics can
accommodate the apparent contradictions within the catuṣkoṭi without triggering the
principle of explosion.

    However, Priest's formalization faces a difficulty. Nāgārjuna's argumentative
method in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (Root Verses of the Middle Way) relies
extensively on modus tollens to frame several series of reductio ad absurdum
arguments - demonstrating that if X had svabhāva, absurd consequences would follow,
and therefore X lacks svabhāva. Under Priest's FDE (First Degree Entailment), modus
tollens is invalid: from P → Q and ¬Q, one cannot infer ¬P when P may have the value
‘both.’1

    The modal extension offered here resolves this tension. By situating the four values
within a modal framework, the reasoning about necessity claims operates classically.

Modus tollens is preserved at the level where Nāgārjuna actually employs it.2 Of course,
we do not claim Nāgārjuna reasoned in terms of possible worlds; we are claiming his
arguments exhibit a structure that modal logic makes perspicuous, just as formalizing
any natural-language argument in logic doesn't impute the formalism to the original
author.

    It should be noted that the formula does not impose modal structure on non-modal
arguments. Svabhāva is constitutively modal: to possess svabhāva is to possess one's
nature independently, intrinsically, not in dependence on conditions - which is to
possess it necessarily. When Nāgārjuna concludes that X lacks svabhāva, he concludes
that X does not have its nature necessarily. The formula makes explicit the modal
structure already present in svabhāva-discourse rather than adding something foreign
to the original arguments.

    This interpretation is not merely one option among equals. Nāgārjuna demonstrably
employs modus tollens throughout the MMK. Readings that treat svabhāva non-
modally render these arguments invalid under four-valued semantics. The modal
reading preserves them. The standard of charitable interpretation in which we attribute
logical competence to the author favors the reading under which Nāgārjuna's explicit
argumentative moves remain valid.

    For instance, consider MMK 2:5-6. Nāgārjuna argues: if the act of going inherently
exists in the path being traversed, then two acts of going would follow - the going that
makes the path 'being traversed,' and the going supposedly located there (Siderits &
Katsura 2013, 34-35) (Translating from the original Sanskrit). Two acts of going entail
two goers. But two goers are absurd; there is one motion and one mover. Therefore,
going does not inherently exist in the path.

    Reconstructed modally, representing entailments that preserve necessity:

    (1) □(v(going-in-path) = t) → □(v(two-goings) = t)
    (2) □(v(two-goings) = t) → □(v(two-goers) = t)
    (3) ¬□(v(two-goers) = t) [there is contingently one goer; necessity fails]
    (4) ∴ ¬□(v(going-in-path) = t)

    Modus tollens applies twice, classically, at the modal level. The four-valued
structure permits the object-level propositions to receive any value; what the argument
refutes is the necessity of those assignments. This is the argumentative pattern
throughout the MMK.

We demonstrate that the formula is satisfiable across the standard hierarchy of normal
modal logics.

3.1 SATISFIABILITY IN K

Consider the following Kripke model M = ⟨W, R, V⟩:

    • W = {w₀, w₁, w₂, w₃, w₄}
    • R = {(w₀, w₁), (w₀, w₂), (w₀, w₃), (w₀, w₄)}
    • V(P, w₁) = t; V(P, w₂) = f; V(P, w₃) = b; V(P, w₄) = n

    At w₀, for each value x ∈ {t, f, b, n}, there exists an accessible world wᵢ such that
V(P, wᵢ) ≠ x. V(P, w₀) may be assigned any value; the accessibility to w₁–w₄ provides
the necessary counterexamples regardless. Therefore:

    • ¬□(v(P) = t) holds at w₀ (since V(P, w₂) ≠ t)
    • ¬□(v(P) = f) holds at w₀ (since V(P, w₁) ≠ f)
    • ¬□(v(P) = b) holds at w₀ (since V(P, w₁) ≠ b)
    • ¬□(v(P) = n) holds at w₀ (since V(P, w₁) ≠ n)

    As the conjunction holds, the formula is satisfiable in K.

3.2 EXTENSION TO T, S4, S5

From the preceding, one then sees how the formula remains satisfiable in T, S4, and S5
modal logics.

    • Under T in which □φ → φ, the additional reflexivity adds accessible worlds and does
         not remove them, keeping the formula satisfiable.

    • Under S4 in which □φ → □□φ, the addition of transitivity chaining world accessibility
         does not affect the formula, as transitivity governs which worlds access which and does
         not touch the values those worlds contain. In other words, the transitivity of
         accessibility does not force the fixity of any value.

    • Under S5, the strongest system, in which □φ ↔ ◇□φ and ◇φ ↔ □◇φ, worlds partition
         into equivalence classes with full mutual accessibility. However, mutual accessibility
         does not entail uniform valuation. A model with diverse valuations across worlds
         within a single equivalence class remains well-formed and satisfies the formula. The
         model constructed for K above, with R extended to an equivalence relation over W,
         satisfies the formula in S5. While T(P) itself is satisfiable in S5, the iteration T(T(P))
         is not; this restriction and its philosophical significance are discussed in Section 2.2.
         (See Appendix for formal verification of T(P) satisfiability in K, T, S4, S5, and
         satisfiability of T(T(P)) in K, T, S4.)

3.3 NEIGHBORHOOD AND NON-NORMAL SEMANTICS

Non-normal semantics are weaker than the above systems in that they validate fewer
necessity inferences. Intuitively, if the formula is satisfiable in the minimal normal
modal logic of K, it remains satisfiable in systems more permissive toward when
necessity fails. For example, in neighborhood semantics, □φ holds at w iff the

Comparative Philosophy 17.2 (2026)      CHAPMAN
                                    11

proposition expressed by φ belongs to the neighborhood N(w) – a designated set of
propositions deemed necessary at that world. If models exist in K where no value is
necessitated, analogous models exist in neighborhood semantics where no value-
assignment proposition belongs to the neighborhood of necessities.

    Similarly, non-normal modal logics weaken the inference rules governing □. Since
our result requires only that necessity claims can fail, not that they must, weaker
systems preserve satisfiability.

    A full treatment is beyond the scope of this paper, but we assert the structural
element: the formula's satisfiability does not depend on features peculiar to normal
modal logic.

3.4 SIGNIFICANCE

By maintaining satisfiability across a broad array of modal systems, both normal and
non-normal, the formula does not merely hold as an artifact of system choice. This is
due to the nature of modal systems: refutation of necessity makes minimal
commitments, and systems generally distinguish themselves by which positive
necessity claims they validate, not by which necessity-denials they permit.

    The iteration’s sensitivity to S5, discussed in Section 2.2, does not undercut this.
On the contrary: the iteration holds in every modal system that admits asymmetric
accessibility and fails only in the system that imposes the uniform necessity svabhāva
is defined against. The instability the formula identifies is a structural feature across
exactly the formal choices appropriate to modeling dependence. This parallels the
pervasiveness of śūnyatā on all levels of conceptual analysis where dependence
operates. A Lean 4 formalization verifying these results is detailed in the Appendix.

                              4. ADDRESSING OBJECTIONS

A position should be considered and defended from the viewpoint of the strongest
identifiable objections. These are identified as follows:

4.1 THE TRIVIALISM CONCERN

The formula and its implications might be read at first glance as overly nihilistic in
suggesting non-necessity to all truth value claims. However, it must be noted that the
formula does not affirmatively deny the presence of valuations and is solely focused
on the modal status of such valuations. This parallels the Buddhist concession that
conventional function is not nonexistent. The nihilist watchword of “anything goes” is
subjected to the formula as a matter of course and revealed to be not necessary, allowing
for pragmatic response.

4.2 THE TRANSCENDENTAL OBJECTION

It is possible for a critic to attempt to attack the formula on what seems to be its own

Comparative Philosophy 17.2 (2026)      CHAPMAN
                                                  12

grounds by noting that to state the formula presupposes semantic stability. However,
we assert that it does not presuppose stability in the sense of refusing or failing to
subject that stability to analysis and refutation. Instead, the observation is embraced as
an unavoidable consequence of the structure: the formula, applied to itself, is not
necessarily true in all possible worlds. Indeed, this unavoidable presupposition of stable
referents, fixed meanings, and determinate boundaries is precisely what Madhyamaka
identifies as svabhāva. Every proposition, insofar as it functions semantically, encodes
this presupposition. The formula therefore applies to any proposition, not merely to
explicitly metaphysical claims.

    This relates to the ‘no thesis’ statement of Nāgārjuna, to be discussed in section 5:
the critic’s own assertion that semantic stability is necessarily presupposed is itself a
necessity claim, subject to the same analysis.

4.3 REFLEXIVE SCOPE

A unique element of the formula’s operation is that any objection to the formula must
itself be expressible as a proposition; any such proposition is subject to the formula’s
analysis. Critics engaging with this framework are therefore obligated to specify what
kind of claim their objection is making and why that claim is not reachable by the
operation it purports to refute. Objections that cannot meet this requirement have not
engaged the framework, as they have instead asserted its inadequacy without showing
the inadequacy’s content. Indeed, objections, in their structure, create binary divides
between permissibility and impermissibility, or accessibility and inaccessibility. Such
propositional binaries are how svabhāva-like necessity claims are formed in
conventional speech. As such, dialectical engagement with necessity-enforcing
binaries, such as through Nāgārjuna's use of reductio ad absurdum, collapses them
into incoherence.

5. THE VIGRAHAVYĀVARTANĪ DEFENSE

Nāgārjuna defends his approach, as outlined in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, in the
Vigrahavyāvartanī. A much-discussed passage within the Vigrahavyāvartanī is as
follows:

    29. If I had any thesis, that fault would apply to me. But I do not have any thesis, so there
    is indeed no fault for me.
    If I had any thesis, the earlier fault you mentioned would apply to me, because the mark of
    my thesis has been affected. But I do not have any thesis. To that extent, while all things
    are empty, completely pacified, and by nature free from substance, from where could a
    thesis come? In this context your statement “there is precisely that fault for you, because
    the mark of your thesis has been affected” is not tenable (Westerhoff 2010, 30).

    This passage has long vexed commentators on Nāgārjuna because it seems to itself
assert a thesis – the thesis of not having a thesis. Scholars have proposed semantic

                                    13

(Westerhoff 2010; Siderits & Garfield 2013), pragmatic (Tillemans 2016), and
dialetheist (Deguchi, Garfield & Priest 2008) readings, among others.

    However, each proposed reading has generated its own tensions and logical issues
in turn. As previously discussed, dialetheist and paraconsistent readings cannot easily
accommodate Nāgārjuna’s extensive usage of modus tollens in argument. Semantic
readings distinguish levels of discourse between the conventional and ultimate truth
without claiming Nāgārjuna rejects assertions, but in so doing risk either infinite regress
in search of ground (“The ultimate truth is that there is no ultimate truth”), or make
‘conventional’ speech dependent on a metaphysically existent “real” to function
(Ferraro 2013). 3 Pragmatic readings emphasize Nāgārjuna’s usage of reductio
arguments (known in Indian philosophy as prasaṅga) to refute positions without
offering theses, but lack the formalization to offer justification of the status of his “no
thesis” statement.

    Our formula neither asserts a metaphysical existent nor evades the content of
Nāgārjuna’s maneuver. A subtle distinction must be highlighted: Semantic readings
must take infinite regress as a problem due to their reliance on positive assertions
regarding conventional and ultimate truth, which presupposes a stable ground from
which no more assertions can productively be made, indicative of svabhāva. In this
sense, the circularity is vicious.

    Our formula is non-vicious, or virtuous; ground is not sought, because the formula's
regress demonstrates ground is unavailable.

    More directly: A regress is vicious when each stage requires completion of
subsequent stages for its justification; absent termination, no stage is justified. The
formula's regress is virtuous because no stage claims justification from subsequent
stages. Each application of T is self-contained. The infinite continuation demonstrates
a structural feature (the unavailability of ground) rather than constituting a failure to
find it.

    In this sense non-termination is the result rather than a defect to be remedied. Non-
termination prevents stabilization into a meta-position and parallels the Buddhist
doctrine of the conceptual proliferation of dualisms within the mind. Nāgārjuna does
not seek to step outside of this proliferation in order to assert a metaphysical existent
that lies outside of mind. Rather, lacking the specific formalism seen in this paper, he
applies the method of the formula to all svabhāva-propositions so as to deconstruct the
processes of conceptual thought within the inescapable language of conceptual thought.

Nāgārjuna is not making a metaphysical assertion but is instead expressing one step of
the self-reflexive prasaṅga methodology. His defense in the Vigrahavyāvartanī
expounds on this.

    “All is real, or all is unreal, all is both real and unreal, all is neither unreal nor real;
this is the graded teaching of the Buddha.” MMK 18:8 (ibid, 200)

    On paper, this seems in contradiction to the formula, as Nāgārjuna seems to be
expressing a positive assertion of all four lemmas being existents. However, operative
here is the descriptor that “this is the graded teaching of the Buddha” – The Buddha
employs pedagogical structure as skillful means that does not contradict the formula,
because a teaching does not necessarily impart a metaphysical existent, nor is it itself
necessarily true. More directly, all four lemmas are held to be available rather than
necessary.

    Moreover, the objection that you make concerning emptiness cannot be a faulty
    consequence for us or for emptiness.
    All is possible when emptiness is possible.
    Nothing is possible when emptiness is impossible.
    You, throwing your own faults on us,
    are like the person mounted on a horse who forgets the horse.
    If you look upon existents as real intrinsically,
    in that case you regard existents as being without cause and conditions.
    Effect and cause, as well as agent, instrument and act,
    arising and ceasing, and fruit – all these you thereby deny.
    Dependent origination we declare to be emptiness.
    It [emptiness] is a dependent concept; just that is the middle path.
    (MMK 24:13-18) (ibid, 276-277)

    Here, Nāgārjuna denies an intrinsic necessity to emptiness, dependent origination,
and the Middle Way itself. As such, the Way rejects a position between extremes or
further fifth truth-value outside of the tetralemma, as Priest has suggested to resolve

the paradox of ineffability (The Fifth Corner of Four, 2018).4 The inexhaustible nature
of the formula expresses the dependency that characterizes dependent origination.

    We can see from these examples that the formula’s framework of modal non-
necessity coherently ties together multiple difficult passages of the MMK into a unified
interpretation. Whether this coherence extends to subsequent transmissions, such as
into Chan Buddhism and Tibetan scholastic traditions, remains to be investigated.

                                        7. CONCLUSION

This paper has presented a modal extension of Belnap-Dunn semantics that formalizes
the Madhyamaka catuṣkoṭi while preserving modus tollens. The formula's self-
application structurally instantiates Nāgārjuna's 'no thesis' defense without evasion or
paradox within the modal systems appropriate to asymmetric dependence. The base
formula's satisfiability across K, T, S4, and S5 demonstrates that the core result is not
an artifact of formal choices, while the iteration’s restriction to non-uniform modalities
reveals that S5’s uniform necessity is structurally incompatible with the directional
grounding characterizing svabhāva – the very target of Nāgārjuna's analysis.

    Madhyamaka, rendered in the language of Western logic, awaits further
investigation when extended to all propositions, given that semanticity depends on the
use of stable referents, fixed meanings, and determinate boundaries that must
presuppose svabhāva in order to perform productive work.
